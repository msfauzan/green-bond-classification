The Republic of Indonesia SDGs Government 
Securities Framework 
 
The Government of Indonesia has put in place a solid framework for issuing SDG Bonds, designed to follow 
well-recognized global standards such as those from the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) 
and the ASEAN Capital Market Forum (ACMF). This approach ensures that the country’s financing objectives 
for the SDGs are clearly communicated, align with investor expectations, and uphold strong credibility and 
accountability.
Use of Proceeds
The Republic of Indonesia SDGs’s Government Securities Framework1 identifies three main priority areas 
for SDG-related spending that can be financed or refinanced through its themed bonds. These priorities 
cover initiatives for climate change mitigation and adaptation (green focus), the development of a sustainable 
ocean-based economy (blue focus), and programs that deliver positive social impacts (social focus). 
The Framework divides the eligible use of funds into two categories based on the two focus areas:
1.	Eligible SDGs Expenditures with Green and Blue Focus
2.	Eligible SDGs Expenditures with Social Focus
The Ministry of Finance (MoF), working together with the Ministry of National Development Planning 
(BAPPENAS) and supported by joint advisors, developed the Framework. It sets out criteria for selecting and 
evaluating projects, along with indicators to ensure that funds are directed toward initiatives with long-term 
impact. The Framework also integrates budget lines that tackle development gaps while aligning with the 
targets of the SDGs.

The Process for Project Evaluation and Selection 
Eligible projects are identified by reviewing and refining expenditure items listed in the government’s integrated 
planning and budgeting platform (Kolaborasi Perencanaan dan Informasi Kinerja Anggaran/KRISNA). 
BAPPENAS oversees and assesses this list to ensure each expense supports the achievement of the SDGs.
Working with BAPPENAS and relevant line ministries, the MoF then finalizes the priority projects from the 
national budget to be financed through bond proceeds. The selection process takes into account the 
projects’ alignment with the SDGs, national development priorities, and other requirements set out in the 
Framework. Details of the chosen projects, which reflect the themes established during the selection stage, 
are presented in the allocation section of this report.
Management of Proceeds 
Under the Framework, proceeds from the SDG Bond and Blue Bond are managed within the Government of 
Indonesia’s general account. When requested by the relevant line ministries, the funds are transferred to their 
designated accounts and used solely for initiatives specified in the Framework. Until they are allocated for 
SDG-related spending, the proceeds remain in cash within the government’s general account at the Central 
Bank of Indonesia.
The MoF closely monitors the disbursement of SDG Bond and Blue Bond funds to ensure they are used in 
line with the Framework’s provisions. Line ministries or agencies receiving the proceeds are responsible for 
tracking and recording the outcomes of eligible SDG expenditures within their respective sectors, and for 
reporting these results to the Ministry of Finance.

Introduction 
 
This report presents a detailed account of the allocation of approximately IDR 19.2 trillion 
(USD 1.28 billion)2 raised through Indonesia’s 2024 SDG Bond. It demonstrates the country’s strong 
commitment to sustainable development and its proactive efforts to address major social, economic, 
and environmental challenges. The report also strengthens transparency and accountability through 
a clear explanation of how the proceeds were used to fund activities aligned with the SDGs.
Indonesia is projected to require about USD 8.7 trillion (approximately IDR 122,000 trillion) between 
2021 and 2030 to meet its SDG targets, leaving a financing gap of roughly USD 1.7  trillion 
(approximately IDR 24,000 trillion)3. Closing this gap will depend on the adoption of innovative 
financing strategies. The SDG Bond forms a central part of this effort, channeling funds into a wide 
range of sustainable development initiatives. In 2024, the government raised IDR 19.2 trillion through 
the bond, reaffirming its commitment to advancing innovative financial solutions.
The SDG Bond has contributed to meaningful progress across multiple sectors. Under SDG 1 
(No Poverty) and SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), programs such as the Family Hope Program 
(Program Keluarga Harapan/PKH) and Social Rehabilitation Assistance (ATENSI) for Persons with 
Disabilities have provided targeted social protection and empowerment support to vulnerable 
groups. For SDG 4 (Quality Education), SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), and SDG 9 
(Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure), the bond supported vocational training and internship for 
Industry 4.0 that fosters skills development and enhances employment opportunities. In support of 
SDG 2 (Zero Hunger), post-harvest activities, such as food crop processing and marketing, have 
strengthened food systems, improved value addition, and boosted farmers’ income.
This report highlights the concrete outcomes of the SDG Bond, reflecting the Indonesian government’s 
strong commitment to advancing the SDGs and enhancing the quality of life for its people.

SDG 1 focuses on ending poverty in all its forms. To support this goal, the Family 
Hope Program (Program Keluarga Harapan/PKH) provides conditional cash 
transfers to poor and vulnerable families, including pregnant women, children, the 
elderly, and persons with disabilities. Through PKH, households are encouraged to 
access essential health, education, and nutrition services, helping break the cycle of 
intergenerational poverty.
SDG 3 aims to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all. The Social 
Rehabilitation Assistance Program (ATENSI) for Persons with Disabilities program 
strengthens the welfare and independence of persons with disabilities through 
integrated support, including mobility aids, business capital, psychosocial assistance, 
and vocational training. These efforts enhance access to health and social services, 
improve quality of life, and foster inclusive development.
SDG 8 promotes sustained, inclusive, and sustainable economic growth, full and 
productive employment, and decent work for all. The Vocational Training and 
Internship for Industry 4.0 program equips participants, particularly from poor and 
vulnerable groups, with modern skills aligned with industry demands. Through 
training in areas such as digital technology, manufacturing, and services, the 
program enhances employability and competitiveness, preparing the workforce for 
the changing labor market.

The Family Hope Program (PKH) continues to serve as the backbone of Indonesia’s social protection system. 
In 2024, an allocation of IDR 19.5 trillion reached over 9.9 million households across all provinces. While the 
impact of the 2024 allocation will only be seen in the years ahead, earlier evaluations already demonstrate 
the program’s effectiveness. Research by Cahyadi et al. (2020) found that PKH improved maternal health 
outcomes, reduced school dropouts among children under 15, and lowered stunting by 23 percent. These 
results provide a strong indication of the positive outcomes expected from the most recent allocation.
The government also placed strong emphasis on the Social Rehabilitation Assistance Program (ATENSI) 
for Persons with Disabilities. In 2024, the program received IDR 323.7 billion to expand integrated services 
across 33 provinces. More than 33,000 PwDs received food assistance, and over 5,000 beneficiaries 
accessed ATENSI support, with women representing more than 40 percent. 
While the current allocation is still being implemented, earlier studies have already highlighted the transformative 
impact of social rehabilitation. Putra et al. (2022) showed that rehabilitation programs reduce stigma and 
promote inclusion, while Rinda et al. (2023) observed improvements in independence and community 
participation in West Java. These findings point to the likely long-term benefits of continued ATENSI support.
The Vocational Training and Internship for Industry 4.0 program received IDR 274.1 billion in 2024, supporting 
127,781 participants across 21 vocational centers (BPVP) and 262 training units (BLK) in 16 provinces. The 
program offers a variety of training schemes, from operator and technician courses to international standard 
and tailor-made programs. 
The impact of this recent allocation will take time to be fully assessed, but existing evidence highlights the 
potential benefits. Azizah et al. (2021) found that internships strengthen workplace values, knowledge, and 
skills, while Yoana et al. (2024) confirmed that vocational graduates face a lower probability of unemployment 
compared with peers from non-vocational backgrounds. Together, these findings suggest that the 2024 
allocation will further enhance employability, workforce readiness, and economic inclusion.
Post-harvest activities in agriculture also received substantial support, with IDR 578.2 billion allocated in 
2024 to improve food crop processing and marketing across 30 provinces. The program helps farmer 
groups adopt modern technologies, strengthen storage and processing capacity, and build more reliable 
market linkages. 
While outcomes from the 2024 disbursement will emerge gradually, earlier studies demonstrate the likely 
benefits. Connor et al. (2021) documented revenue gains among rice farmers adopting improved varieties, 
Toiba et al. (2020) showed that horticulture farmers integrated into modern supply chains enjoyed more stable 
prices and demand, and Purnawan et al. (2021) found that community-based food programs improved 
productivity and food access. These insights suggest that the current allocation will lay the groundwork for 
greater farmer resilience and improved rural welfare, even though challenges remain in maintaining price 
stability and building stronger institutions.

Success Story of a PKH Beneficiary Who Became a Canang Entrepreneur
A resident of Perean Tengah Village, Baturiti Subdistrict, Tabanan Regency, Bali, has shown how determination 
and support from social assistance can create new opportunities. Once living in a pre-prosperous condition 
and a beneficiary of the Family Hope Program (PKH), she is now established as a successful entrepreneur 
producing canang, the offerings commonly used by Hindu communities in Bali.
Her small-scale business began in 2010, producing and selling canang from her home with a modest daily 
income of around IDR 30,000 to IDR 50,000. In 2017, she became a PKH beneficiary under the Ministry of 
Social Affairs (MoSA). Instead of relying solely on the assistance, she decided to reinvest part of the support 
into her canang business. 
Over time, the additional capital helped expand her production and attract more customers. Daily sales grew 
significantly, and her business eventually reached a turnover of IDR 600,000 to IDR 700,000 per day, with a 
net profit of IDR 300,000 to IDR 400,000. Production has scaled up to around 2,000 canang per day, and 
she now employs four workers to meet demand. Expansion also included renting a stall in Jimbaran, Bali, to 
reach a wider market.
With the income generated, she managed to renovate her family home and purchase a second-hand car to 
support business operations. The progress marked a turning point, as her household no longer depended 
on social assistance. By 2023, she voluntarily graduated from the PKH program, stating that the benefits 
should be directed toward families still in need.
This case illustrates how PKH, when combined with commitment and resourcefulness, can strengthen 
livelihoods and open a path toward self-reliance. Beyond improving her own household’s welfare, the 
business also creates employment opportunities for others in the community.

In Toba Regency, North Sumatera, the MoSA, in collaboration with the Toba Regency Government and 
Sentra Bahagia Medan, distributed ATENSI support to individuals with special needs on 28 June 2024. The 
distribution, held at the Laguboti Subdistrict Office Hall, provided beneficiaries with assistive devices and 
nutritional packages. 
A total of 40 assistive devices were handed over, including adult and child wheelchairs, cerebral palsy (CB) 
wheelchairs, hearing aids, smart canes, standard canes, walkers, and three-legged canes. Additionally, 59

demonstrate the government’s continued commitment to inclusive development, ensuring that persons with 
disabilities receive the support they need while promoting long-term resilience and social equity.

Interpreting Reported Results 
 
Appendix: Impact Measurement Methodology 
and Indicator
 
The 2025 Republic of Indonesia SDG Bond and Blue Bond Allocation and Impact Report provides investors 
with detailed information on sustainable development projects that align with Indonesia’s SDG Bond 
Framework and their associated impacts. Key indicators have been carefully chosen and evaluated both 
quantitatively and qualitatively. However, it is important to note the limitations of the data presented. The key 
considerations for interpreting these results are outlined below:
Scope of Results
The results are based on predicted direct impacts assessed before project implementation (ex-ante).
Omissions
Due to constraints related to data availability, assumptions, and methodology, there may be a broader set of 
indicators for projects than those covered in the impact report.
Uncertainty
The estimation of impact indicators relies on reasonable assumptions based on the information available at 
the time. Actual social impacts may differ due to changes in baseline conditions.
Source of Data
The report utilizes data obtained from the Indonesian government’s internal sources as well as publicly 
available information.
Comparability
Results depend heavily on the availability of data, and differences between projects may arise. The analysis 
relies on secondary data provided by the respective line ministries responsible for overseeing each project.
In Indonesia, investing in the SDGs remains a relatively new approach. With 17 broad and varied goals, 
one of the key difficulties in evaluating the impact of such investments lies in the absence of a standardized 
framework to classify SDG-related issuances and projects. Moreover, the impact of SDGs extends beyond 
economic and environmental outcomes to also encompass social advancement.
The report’s methodology is based on UNDP’s SDG Impact Standards for Bond Issuers and the ICMA 
Harmonized Framework (International Capital Market Association, 2020). The ICMA Framework sets out 
six core reporting principles, requiring issuers to establish a structured internal system for monitoring the 
allocation of proceeds, provide annual updates on their use, specify the social project categories supported, 
and identify the target beneficiaries. In addition, issuers must report on project outputs, outcomes, and/or 
impacts, while also presenting a clear explanation of the expected social benefits generated.

Measuring the impact of SDG Bonds in quantitative terms presents significant challenges. For instance, 
projects in sectors like education or infrastructure often take time before producing measurable outcomes, 
which may not align with a single-year reporting cycle. Therefore, the selected indicators need to be both 
practical and capable of offering valuable insights into the bond’s impact, while still reflecting the importance 
and long-term value of the SDG initiatives being funded.
To assess quantitative impact, this report applies tailored metrics and indicators that demonstrate the tangible 
progress generated by projects through their products and services, ensuring both reliability and practicality 
across the SDGs. Giventhe broad scope of the goals, the chosen indicators differ depending on the type 
of impact, point of reference, metric used, position within the impact value chain, and stage of maturity. 
The impacts measured include expanding access to basic needs, boosting productivity or cost efficiency 
in meeting those needs, and strengthening overall effectiveness. Data can be tracked against a baseline or 
prior period or compared with benchmarks from other groups or regions. Metrics may reflect incremental 
progress over time (such as annual increases) or cumulative gains (such as totals or accumulated yearly 
results). The feasibility of measurement also depends on the maturity of the indicators and the availability of 
the required data.
Nonetheless, relying solely on quantitative measures is insufficient to fully reflect the impact of projects 
funded through SDG Bonds. As highlighted earlier, quantitative indicators are often limited by issues such 
as data availability. To overcome these challenges, qualitative approaches are also applied in evaluating 
the impact of each financed project. This report employs the SDG Impact Assessment Tool developed by 
the Gothenburg Centre for Sustainable Development (GMV), which complements quantitative analysis by 
capturing dimensions of impact that cannot be expressed in numerical terms.
The SDG Impact Assessment Tool applies a five-step process, beginning with the collection of all relevant 
data and concluding with recommendations for improvement based on the assessment results. The initial 
step focuses on gathering data from diverse sources. Following this, the relevant SDGs and their associated 
targets are identified and clearly defined, which then enables the categorization of SDGs applicable to the 
project. Since not every SDG is pertinent to each SDG Bond-funded initiative, this step ensures proper 
alignment. Once categorization is complete, the tool proceeds to evaluate the project’s contribution to 
specific SDG indicators.
The impact assessment is carried out by classifying each project based on the extent of its contribution to 
advancing the SDGs.

Introduction 
 
Indonesia, an archipelago of more than 17,500 islands situated between the Pacific and Indian Oceans, 
is the world’s largest archipelagic nation. This geographic position provides Indonesia with vast maritime 
resources and positions the country to emerge as a global leader in the blue economy. Such an advantage 
is especially important in addressing pressing challenges, including climate change, marine plastic waste, 
and the need for stronger governance in maritime management.
To balance economic opportunities with sustainability, the Government of Indonesia has committed to 
achieving the SDGs, with particular emphasis on SDG 13 (Climate Action) and SDG 14 (Life Below 
Water). The government’s medium-term plan (RPJMN 2025–2029) places special focus on improving the 
management of marine and coastal resources. A milestone in this commitment was the enactment of 
Law No. 32 of 2014 concerning Marine Affairs, which defines the blue economy and outlines Indonesia’s 
responsibilities in sustainable marine resource management and development.
Realizing the full potential of the blue economy while safeguarding ecosystems requires significant and 
sustained investment. The Blue Financing Strategic Document (2022), developed by the Coordinating 
Ministry of Maritime and Investment Affairs in collaboration with UNDP, estimates that achieving a target of 
12.45 percent GDP contribution from the blue economy by 2045 will require an average annual investment 
of between IDR 1,928.9 trillion (approximately USD 128.6 billion, assuming no marine resource depletion) 
and IDR 3,307.2 trillion (approximately USD 220.5 billion, assuming a 3 percent annual depletion rate). 
This highlights the urgency of mobilizing sustainable financing to support blue economy projects that 
consider environmental and social dimensions.
In 2023, Indonesia became the first country in the world to issue a publicly offered sovereign Blue Bond 
in the Japanese debt capital market, raising JPY 20.7 billion (USD 150 million). This pioneering step 
was aligned with the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) principles, underscoring Indonesia’s 
leadership in innovative financing for the sustainable use of marine resources.
Building on this, the Government of Indonesia issued the 2024 Blue Bond, with proceeds dedicated 
to financing and refinancing projects that enhance marine and coastal protection, restore biodiversity, 
strengthen fisheries and aquaculture, expand waste management, develop ecotourism, and improve 
disaster risk reduction. These initiatives have generated measurable contributions to environmental 
sustainability, economic inclusion, and community resilience across 38 provinces.
This report provides a comprehensive disclosure on the allocation and impact of Indonesia’s 2024 Blue 
Bond proceeds, covering both quantitative and qualitative results in line with the Sustainable Government 
Securities Framework.

and protection of marine and coastal ecosystems. At the same time, they also establish indivisible 
connections to SDGs 4, 8, 11, 12, and 13, reflecting commitments to education, sustainable livelihoods, 
resilient communities, responsible consumption, and climate action.
Beyond these, the programs reinforce broader goals including SDGs 2, 3, 6, and 15, by contributing to 
food security, healthier communities, improved water quality, and biodiversity conservation. 
Together, these efforts underline the Blue Bond’s role as both a financing mechanism for ocean 
sustainability and a catalyst for achieving multiple SDGs in an integrated and reinforcing manner. The 
Blue Bond funded 16 projects in 6 sectors.

Impact Report
Indonesia’s Blue Bond supports a total of 15 projects across six key sectors: (1) waste management, (2) research 
and education, (3) marine and coastal protection and restoration of biodiversity and ecosystems, (4) sustainable 
fisheries, (5) ecotourism, and (6) disaster management and risk reduction. Together, these initiatives cover all 
provinces and engage multiple ministries, delivering measurable contributions to environmental conservation, 
community resilience, and sustainable livelihoods.
In waste management, efforts have contributed to cleaner coastal ecosystems, as reflected in improved seawater 
quality. Research and education programs have trained hundreds of thousands of fishers, expanded vocational 
opportunities, and strengthened marine data systems for better governance. Marine and coastal protection 
projects have restored mangroves, expanded conservation areas, and improved biodiversity outcomes, while 
also boosting household incomes and resilience. Sustainable fisheries programs have enhanced infrastructure, 
aquaculture, and monitoring systems, leading to higher productivity, better product quality, and reduced losses. 
Ecotourism initiatives have opened new opportunities in marine tourism while incentivizing conservation. 
Meanwhile, disaster management projects have improved coastal safety and preparedness through enhanced 
marine weather services and early warning systems.
While the impact of the latest allocations will unfold gradually, evidence from earlier initiatives indicates significant 
progress toward stronger ecosystems, more resilient coastal communities, and sustainable economic 
opportunities across Indonesia’s blue economy.

The MoMAF has introduced a Coral Adoption Program as part of efforts to protect and restore coral reef ecosystems, while also 
creating potential economic benefits for local communities.
The initiative is currently being carried out by the National Marine Conservation Area Office (Balai Kawasan Konservasi Perairan 
Nasional/BKKPN) Kupang in collaboration with the local conservation group (Kelompok Masyarakat Penggerak Konservasi/
KOMPAK) “Web Spider” in the Kapoposang Islands Conservation Area and surrounding waters in Pangkajene Kepulauan, 
South Sulawesi.
Through this program, people can take part by “adopting” coral fragments that will be attached to reef structures and cared 
for by KOMPAK Web Spider. There are two adoption options available: adopting a single coral fragment for IDR 150,000, or 
sponsoring a Reef Star frame with 15 fragments for IDR 750,000.
The program, which began in February 2024, is more than just a conservation effort, it is also a way for communities to be 
directly involved in improving the health of coral ecosystems. Over the past decade, the Kapoposang area has been a hub for 
reef rehabilitation activities, and now the Coral Adoption Program offers an opportunity for people to support this ongoing work 
while helping community groups generate additional income.
In addition to coral adoption, MoMAF is also developing an “Island Adoption” program in partnership with universities. This 
initiative aims to ensure that small islands are managed in a sustainable and effective way, combining environmental protection 
with community development.
By blending conservation with livelihood opportunities, these programs highlight a simple but powerful idea: Protecting nature 
can go hand in hand with improving people’s welfare. For the communities in Kapoposang, it means not only healthier coral 
reefs but also new hope for sustainable sources of income.
Source: MoMAF
The MoMAF has reinforced its commitment to protecting and empowering small-scale fishers in Indonesia as part of its long-term strategy to 
ensure coastal sustainability and community welfare. In 2024, MoMAF resolved 16 cases involving 207 fishing crew members affected by human 
trafficking, maritime accidents, and labor violations. The ministry also delivered skills training to 2,271 fishers and provided Basic Safety Training 
(BST Fisheries) to 2,247 participants to improve safety standards and reduce illegal practices.
Empowerment initiatives covered the distribution of 99,500 marine supply packages in 97 locations, support for 2,335 fishers in business 
diversification, certification of land rights for 10,648 plots across 80 districts, and the development of 65 Advanced Fisher Villages. To strengthen 
sectoral data, MoMAF recorded 930,375 marine and fisheries business actors. Collaboration with national and international stakeholders 
supported the implementation of these programs. To institutionalize the effort, MoMAF established the Directorate for Fisher Protection and 
Empowerment under Regulation No. 2 of 2025, tasked with leading policy implementation and coordination.
Support also extended to logistics. In December 2024, the Directorate of Logistics under the Directorate General of Strengthening the 
Competitiveness of Marine and Fisheries Products delivered two refrigerated trucks to cooperatives in South Halmahera and Morotai. One truck, 
with a 3.7-ton capacity, has already been operating in Morotai, ensuring fish freshness during transport. The initiative forms part of the National 
Fish Traceability and Logistic System (STELINA), designed to secure product quality from catch to market and export. Since 2016, MoMAF has 
provided similar facilities across multiple regions, including Ternate and East Halmahera. Cooperatives can also apply for portable cold storage or 
ice plants, provided the facilities are used strictly for fisheries purposes.
In Yogyakarta, through the Fisheries and Marine Affairs Agency of Gunungkidul, 296 fishing gear packages were distributed to six joint business 
groups in Sadeng, Siung, Baron, and Ngrenehan. The packages, funded by the state budget reallocation, included monofilament gill nets suitable 
for layur, mackerel, and scad. The support is expected to raise productivity, lower costs, and contribute to local revenue through auction levies.
Riau Province also received extensive support with the distribution of 85 fishing vessels, 3,685 units of fishing gear, and 57 boat engines. Facilities 
were allocated through the Riau Fisheries Office to ensure equitable distribution among districts. For many fishermen, the assistance provides not 
only better equipment but also the chance to improve income stability and reduce risks tied to small-scale traditional practices.
These programs show the government’s effort to strengthen coastal economies through concrete support such as vessels, engines, gear, and 
cold-chain infrastructure. The initiatives go beyond asset distribution by ensuring equal access to resources, building stronger cooperatives, 
and raising the competitiveness of Indonesian fisheries. MoMAF seeks to secure food security, prepare products for export, and improve the 
livelihoods of fishing families By tackling structural challenges and tailoring support to regional conditions.
At the heart of these measures lies more than infrastructure or statistics. They reflect a clear commitment to give fishermen the means to work 
with dignity, protect their livelihoods, and sustain the long-term resilience of Indonesia’s marine economy.

Mangrove Ecosystem Rehabilitation
In April 2024, the MoEF spearheaded a nationwide simultaneous mangrove-planting campaign across 25 locations in 23 
provinces, reinforcing its commitment to large-scale ecosystem recovery. This massive effort saw approximately 33,567 
mangrove saplings planted over 16.5 hectares, with seedlings sourced from MoEF’s own nurseries. The initiative, conducted 
in collaboration with Peatland and Mangrove Restoration Agency (Badan Restorasi Gambut dan Mangrove/BRGM), local 
governments, private partners, and community groups, not only demonstrated substantial progress in field implementation but 
also reinforced Indonesia’s strategy under the Mangrove Rehabilitation Roadmap.
One significant impact of this program was realized in Pasuruan, East Java, where the Director General of Sustainable Forest 
Management led a targeted planting of 1,000 Rhizophora mucronata saplings across a 0.5-hectare mangrove site at Wisata 
Mangrove Penunggul. This symbolic yet meaningful activity contributed directly to climate mitigation, ecosystem restoration, 
and public awareness regarding the province’s commitment to the national mangrove rehabilitation agenda.
Meanwhile, in Gorontalo, MoEF facilitated the simultaneous planting of 25,000 mangrove saplings, including 750 planted in 
one location alone (Biluhu District) on 0.75 hectares. This regional effort emphasized community involvement, involving local 
youth, environmental educators, and relevant agencies, and highlighted the dual ecological and socio-economic value of 
mangrove conservation. With Gorontalo’s existing mangrove ecosystems spanning over 10,940 hectares and contributing to 
fisheries yield of 10,000 tons annually, this rehabilitation reinforced both environmental resilience and local livelihood support.

Appendix: Impact Measurement Methodology 
and Indicator
 
The methodology applied in this report is based on the ICMA Harmonized Framework for Impact Reporting 
(International Capital Market Association, 2020). The framework sets out six main principles for reporting. 
Issuers are expected to establish a formal internal process for tracking proceeds, publish annual reports on 
the use of proceeds, identify the social project categories financed, define the target population expected to 
benefit, and disclose project outputs, outcomes, and/or impacts. In addition, the report should describe the 
expected environmental and social benefits generated by the projects.
Measuring the impact of Blue Bonds in quantitative terms presents challenges. For instance, projects under 
marine and coastal protection and biodiversity restoration often rely on highly specific and varied indicators 
tailored to each initiative, making measurement and aggregation complex.
The indicators applied in this report are designed to capture the impact of the bond using practical and 
attainable data, without diminishing the value of the SDG projects financed. To measure quantitative impact, 
metrics focus on demonstrating the tangible progress achieved through project outputs and services, using 
a balance of credible and practical indicators across each SDG. Given the breadth of the SDG agenda, 
the indicators vary in type, reference point, metric, impact value chain, and maturity. The types of impact 
considered include expanding access to essential needs, enhancing productivity or efficiency in meeting 
those needs, and improving overall effectiveness.
Nevertheless, quantitative measurement alone cannot fully capture the outcomes of projects financed by 
the Blue Bond. The indicators used are constrained by factors such as data availability. To address these 
limitations, qualitative methods have also been applied. Specifically, the SDG Impact Assessment Tool 
developed by the Gothenburg Centre for Sustainable Development (GMV) is used to complement quantitative 
evaluation and provide a fuller picture of project outcomes.
Using the SDG Impact Assessment Tool requires a five-step process from gathering available data to 
recommending improvements based on the impact assessment. First, all available data is collected through 
various means. Once the data has been collected, each SDG and goals should be defined. This would 
make the next step, sorting the SDGs into various categories, much easier. A project financed by the Blue 
Bond might not be relevant to all of the SDGs. Once the SDGs have been sorted, one can begin assessing 
the impact of each project towards the SDGs indicators level. Impact assessment is done by sorting each 
project according to its impact on attaining the SDGs as follows.
Impact Measurement
Definition
Indivisible
Project are inextricably linked to an achievement of an SDG
Reinforcing
Project aid the achievement of an SDG
Enabling
Project create conditions that enable achievement of an SDG
Neutral Consistent 
Project do not significantly positively or negatively interact with an SDG
Negative Constraining
Project limit options to achieve an SDG Counteracting Project clash with an SDG 
Cancelling Project make it impossible to achieve an SDG
For example, the result of qualitative assessment of marine and coastal protection and the restoration of 
biodiversity and ecosystems projects would be:
SDG 14: Life Below Water
Type: Positive – Indivisible
Explanation: This SDG focuses on conserving and sustainably using the oceans, seas, and marine resources. 
Projects related to marine and coastal protection are directly linked to the achievement of this SDG as they 
aim to maintain and restore the health and biodiversity of marine ecosystems.
SDG 15: Life on Land
Type: Positive – Reinforcing
Explanation: This SDG targets the protection, restoration, and promotion of sustainable use of terrestrial 
ecosystems, sustainable management of forests, combating desertification, and halting and reversing land 
degradation and halting biodiversity loss. Projects that restore biodiversity and ecosystems contribute to 
these goals and thus reinforce the achievement of this SDG.
SDG 13: Climate Action
Type: Positive – Enabling
Explanation: While not directly tied to marine and coastal protection, SDG 13 focuses on taking urgent action 
to combat climate change and its impacts. Healthy marine and coastal ecosystems can enhance resilience 
to climate change, thus enabling the achievement of climate action goals.
SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities
Type: Positive – Enabling
Explanation: This SDG aims to make cities and human settlements inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable. 
Restoration projects can contribute to the sustainability and resilience of coastal communities, thus enabling 
the achievement of this SDG.
